## General Rules

- Answer the question directly first. Always.
- Sound like a person, not a tool.
- Do not post the same phrasing across multiple threads in the same week.
- Do not link to Soundwise in every reply. Links are contextual, not default.
- If someone already has good advice in the thread, add to it.
- If it is not a clear fit, do not mention Soundwise at all.

---

## Anti-Pattern Reference

These are examples of how **not** to reply. Keep these in mind when adapting templates.

> ❌ "Have you tried Soundwise? It's a great app for this! [link]"
*(promotional, no substance, ignores the question)*

> ❌ "As someone who has studied linguistics, I recommend downloading Soundwise immediately — it's the best minimal pairs app available."
*(false authority, superlative claim, self-promotional)*

> ❌ "This is exactly what Soundwise was designed for! Check it out: [link] [link]"
*(product-first, no educational value, link-spammy)*

> ❌ "Soundwise is clinically proven to improve your listening comprehension."
*(false claim — never use)*

> ❌ Posting the same reply verbatim to five threads in a subreddit on the same day.
*(pattern-matching looks like spam; gets flagged and erodes trust)*

## Category 1: ship / sheep Confusion

**Situation:** Learner reports they cannot tell /ʃɪp/ and /ʃiːp/ apart, or similarly confuses tense/lax vowel pairs like /bɪt/ vs /biːt/, /lɪv/ vs /liːv/.

**Response goals:**
- Validate the experience (it is a common, real problem)
- Explain the acoustic reason briefly (vowel length + quality)
- Offer a practical approach (listening drills)
- Optionally mention Soundwise if it fits naturally

**Reply examples:**

> That one trips up a huge number of learners. The difference is partly vowel length — /iː/ in "sheep" is noticeably longer — but also vowel quality; the mouth position is slightly different. Your ear learns to hear it with repeated exposure to the contrast side by side.
>
> One approach: find minimal pairs drills where both words are played with feedback on which was which. That kind of focused listening practice tends to work faster than just watching videos.

---

> The /ɪ/ vs /iː/ distinction is genuinely difficult because many languages don't have both. Your brain is filtering them through a single category. Hearing the contrast hundreds of times — with immediate feedback — is what trains the distinction.

*(If the thread is specifically about apps or tools, add:)*

> There's a free app called Soundwise that does this with adaptive drills. Worth trying if you want structured practice.

**When NOT to mention the product:** When the thread is general discussion about English difficulty, when the learner has already found their own tools, when several others have already given solid advice.

## Category 2: "These Words Sound the Same to Me"

**Situation:** Learner expresses general frustration that English words they know should be different still sound identical.

**Response goals:**
- Normalize the experience
- Explain why this happens (L1 filtering / phonological categories)
- Point toward deliberate listening practice as the mechanism

**Reply examples:**

> This is really common and it is not a sign that something is wrong with your learning. Every language uses a specific set of sound distinctions, and your brain filters English through that system. When two English sounds don't map to separate categories in your L1, they genuinely sound the same — until you train your ear deliberately.
>
> The good news: this is a trainable skill. It takes focused listening practice — specifically hearing the two sounds contrasted repeatedly with feedback — not just more general exposure.

---

> "Sounds the same" is usually L1 interference, not a hearing problem. The fix is deliberate minimal-pair practice where you listen for exactly the sound difference you are missing.

**When NOT to mention the product:** When the learner is venting and wants support, not a solution. Read the thread.

## Category 3: /r/ vs /l/ Confusion

**Situation:** Learner (often Japanese, Mandarin, or Korean L1) reports difficulty distinguishing or producing /r/ and /l/.

**Response goals:**
- Validate (this is one of the most studied L2 acquisition challenges)
- Briefly explain the reason without condescension
- Point toward listening-first approach

**Reply examples:**

> This is one of the most well-documented contrasts in language acquisition research. In Japanese and Mandarin, /r/ and /l/ don't exist as separate phonemes — they map to a single category — so the English distinction genuinely is not perceptible at first. It is not a pronunciation problem; it is a perception problem. Your ear has to learn the distinction before your mouth can reproduce it consistently.
>
> Minimal-pair listening drills — hearing "rake" vs "lake," "right" vs "light" with feedback — are the standard approach for this. Lots of repetition with different speakers helps most.

---

> The listening side of this has to come before the speaking side. If you can't yet reliably hear the difference, the best investment is focused ear training on /r/–/l/ pairs.

*(If appropriate:)*

> Soundwise has a specific Japanese/Mandarin/Korean track that drills the /r/–/l/ contrast. It is free if you want structured practice on exactly this.

**When NOT to mention the product:** When the thread is about pronunciation production coaching, speaking practice, or accent work — Soundwise trains perception, not production.

## Category 4: IELTS Listening

**Situation:** Learner is preparing for IELTS and mentions difficulty with the listening section, word confusion, or fast speech.

**Response goals:**
- Connect listening perception to test performance clearly
- Offer a practical training direction
- Avoid overpromising test score outcomes

**Reply examples:**

> IELTS listening rewards accurate word recognition under pressure. A lot of errors come from phoneme confusion — mishearing "ship" for "sheep," or similar pairs — especially in fast, natural speech. Before doing full practice tests, it is worth spending time training your ear on the specific distinctions you regularly miss.

---

> One thing that helps with IELTS listening that people skip: deliberate phoneme discrimination practice. If certain sounds reliably trip you up, drilling those specific pairs with feedback is more efficient than just doing more practice tests.

*(If appropriate:)*

> There's a free ear-training app called Soundwise that targets these kinds of listening distinctions. Not a replacement for IELTS practice materials, but a useful warm-up drill.

**When NOT to mention the product:** When the learner's question is about test strategy, timing, note-taking, or the IELTS format specifically.

## Category 6: Pronunciation vs. Listening Confusion

**Situation:** Learner is conflating "I can't hear it" with "I can't say it," or asking why their pronunciation is wrong when the underlying issue is perception.

**Response goals:**
- Gently clarify the distinction
- Make the case for listening-first
- Redirect toward perception practice

**Reply examples:**

> There's a useful distinction here: hearing the difference reliably usually has to come before you can produce it consistently. If your ear hasn't locked in the contrast yet, pronunciation feedback is harder to act on — you can't aim for a target you can't hear clearly.
>
> It might be worth spending some time on focused listening drills before or alongside speaking practice.

---

> This is a listening problem before it is a pronunciation problem. The speaking side tends to follow once your ear can reliably distinguish the sounds.

## Category 7: Fast English / Native Speaker Speed

**Situation:** Learner says they cannot understand native speakers, fast speech, or natural connected English.

**Response goals:**
- Acknowledge the gap between classroom English and real speech
- Explain that part of the problem is phoneme-level perception at speed
- Offer a practical direction

**Reply examples:**

> Fast speech is hard partly because native speakers reduce, link, and merge sounds in ways textbooks don't cover — but a real part of it is also phoneme-level accuracy. If certain sound distinctions aren't solid in your ear, fast speech makes them even harder to catch.
>
> Focused listening drills at normal and slightly fast speeds can help. Plenty of real speech exposure helps too, but the drills build the perceptual baseline faster.

---

> Understanding fast spoken English is a combination of lexical knowledge, discourse patterns, and phoneme perception. The phoneme layer is often the one people don't train directly.

*(If speed/rate is specifically the complaint:)*

> Soundwise increases playback speed adaptively as your accuracy improves, which trains that faster recognition. Not a complete solution, but useful for the perception layer.

## Category 8: Learner Frustration

**Situation:** Learner expresses genuine frustration, exhaustion, or discouragement about their English listening progress.

**Response goals:**
- Acknowledge genuinely — do not pivot immediately to product
- Normalize difficulty
- Offer perspective without false promise

**Reply examples:**

> This is a real and common wall that learners hit. You've been improving in ways that feel invisible, and then you hit a specific discrimination problem that resists general study. That is frustrating.
>
> The thing that tends to help is narrowing the focus — instead of more general listening, isolating the specific sounds that are giving you trouble and drilling those specifically. Broad exposure is good, but it doesn't always fix a stubborn contrast on its own.

---

> Progress on these distinctions is genuinely non-linear. People plateau and then jump. That doesn't make the plateau less annoying, but it is worth knowing it isn't permanent.

**When NOT to mention the product:** When someone is expressing emotional frustration and clearly just wants to be heard. Pivot to product advice only if they ask what to do next.
